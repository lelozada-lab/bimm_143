Background

All functions in R have at least 3 things:

  • a name (we pick that and use it to call the function)
  • input arguments (one or more comma seperated inputs that go inside the brackets when we call the function)
  • the body (the lines of R code that do the work of the function)

Q5b. Speculate why very short random peptides are almost always found in nr, while longer ones typically are not. Your answer should refer both to the size of the sequence space (20𝐿 for a peptide of length 𝐿) and to the size of the known protein universe. [1 pt]

Short peptides have fewer possible amino acid combinations, making them more likely to match sequences in the large database of know peptides. As peptide length increases, the number of possible sequences increases exponentially. Because there are many possible longer sequences compared to the number of known proteins, longer random peptides are less likely to match existing proteins.

Q6. In 3–6 sentences total and using your Q5 data and the reasoning from Q5b, what do you think this minimum length is and why might it be a bad design choice for the immune system to present very short peptides? [3 pt]

Based on my Q5 results, I think the minimum peptide length is 8-9 amino acids. My results show that peptides with 6-7 amino acids match existing proteins, while longer peptides begin to appear unique. Presenting short peptides would be a bad choice because they are more likely to match many different proteins, including the body’s own proteins. Longer peptides can help the immune system tell the difference between the body’s own proteins and unkown proteins.
